Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is one of the most revolutionary developments in medical imaging of recent decades. While traditional imaging methods such as X-ray or CT use radiation to create images, MRI uses a combination of magnetic fields and radiofrequency waves. But how exactly does this technique work? Let's dive into the basics of MRI diagnostics!

### The key: water

Most of the human body is made of water, and this water contains hydrogen atoms. The protons in the nuclei of these atoms are especially sensitive to magnetic fields. When the body is placed in a strong magnetic field, these protons line up in the direction of the field.

Next, we apply a radiofrequency pulse that “knocks” these protons out of their aligned state. Once the radiofrequency pulse stops, the protons return to their original state and release energy as they do. We detect this energy and use it to create the image.

### T1 and T2

We distinguish two basic types of MRI images: T1-weighted and T2-weighted images. T1-weighted images record the time it takes the protons to return to their original state (relaxation time), while T2-weighted images track the energy transfer between protons. Based on this information, different tissues appear differently on MRI images.

MRI is especially useful for imaging soft tissues such as the brain, joints or tendons, because these tissues contain a lot of water and therefore many hydrogen atoms. Unlike CT or X-ray images, MRI does not use ionizing radiation, so it carries no radiation risk.

### Contrast agents and progress

Using contrast agents further increases the contrast and sharpness of MRI images. These special compounds amplify the differences in MRI signal between different tissues, allowing even more accurate identification of pathologies such as tumors or inflammation.

The basics of MRI diagnostics rest on a unique link between physics and biology. The magnetic properties of the hydrogen atoms in the human body make it possible to create extremely detailed and accurate images, which have fundamentally changed medical diagnostics. Magnetic resonance imaging gives deep insight into the inside of the body and allows early and accurate identification of abnormalities without exposing patients to ionizing radiation. Understanding and further developing MRI technology remains at the forefront of medical research, and it will remain an essential tool in patient care for many decades to come.
